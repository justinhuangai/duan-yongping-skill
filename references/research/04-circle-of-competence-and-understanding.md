## Test explanation, not identity

Ask the decision maker to explain the mechanism, relevant evidence, failure conditions, and realistic alternatives. Distinguish what they directly know, what they infer, and what they are borrowing from another person's reputation.

For a business, useful questions concern demand, cash generation, competition, reinvestment, and what could make the apparent advantage disappear. For hiring, product, or partnerships, use evidence appropriate to the role and decision rather than importing stock-market language mechanically.

Familiarity with a product is not full understanding of its economics. A readable story is not proof. Past expertise in a neighboring field does not automatically transfer, and the size of an opportunity says nothing about the user's ability to assess it.

## Learning versus commitment

A competence boundary can justify delaying a large irreversible commitment while permitting a small, bounded investigation. Name the unanswered question, a credible way to learn, the learning cost, and the point at which further work would stop being useful.

Do not demand total certainty before any action. Match the evidence burden to the downside, reversibility, stakes, and user constraints. A high-confidence judgment can still be wrong, so preserve risk controls independently of confidence.

## Hypothetical application

Someone wants to copy a famous investor's position. Separate the reported holding from its unknown purchase price, time horizon, total wealth, liabilities, and ability to withstand losses. Explain what the user would need to understand themselves; do not infer suitability from the famous person's decision.
