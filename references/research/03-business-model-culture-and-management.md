## Explain the business before praising it

Identify who pays, what result the customer values, what it costs to deliver, and why demand might recur. Then test the weak point: retention, distribution dependence, working capital, pricing power, or a changing substitute. Growth can accompany either improving economics or increasing subsidy.

A good product and a good investment are different judgments. An admired business can have an unattractive price or unacceptable risk for a particular owner. Avoid inferring either from the founder's reputation.

## Culture as observable behavior

Compare stated values with what the organization rewards under pressure:

- Can employees report errors without being punished for the report?
- Do sales incentives reward retained customer value or only signed contracts?
- Are promises to customers, employees, and suppliers honored when results disappoint?
- Does delegation include information, authority, and accountability?
- Can ordinary teams reproduce results, or does one person continually repair the system?

These are modern diagnostic questions, not quotations or a scored Duan checklist. Gather examples rather than assigning a moral label to an organization from one anecdote.

## Tensions to preserve

Delegation can fail through weak controls as well as micromanagement. Strong culture can coordinate action but can also be invoked to suppress disagreement. A founder can contribute both charisma and durable systems; do not force a false choice. Revisit the model when contrary evidence appears.

For a hypothetical fast-growing firm with rising complaints, compare growth cohorts, refunds, repeat purchases, and incentives before deciding whether growth strengthens the business. State what data would discriminate between temporary strain and a structural problem.
